CPSC eFiling, explained in plain English for small sellers

1. What is CPSC eFiling?

eFiling is the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission's requirement that importers of regulated consumer products submit certificate-of-compliance data electronically — through U.S. Customs and Border Protection's ACE system — instead of handing over paper certificates when asked.

The rule lives in 16 CFR 1110. Before eFiling, a Children's Product Certificate (CPC) or General Certificate of Conformity (GCC) was a document you kept on file and produced on demand. Now the underlying data — who certified, what was tested, which lab, which rules — must be transmitted as structured data tied to your customs entry, before your goods clear.

2. Who has to file?

If you import consumer products that fall under a CPSC rule, ban, standard, or regulation, you file. That includes:

  • Children's products (toys, kids' apparel, nursery products, children's jewelry) — the biggest category for small sellers.
  • Products subject to specific CPSC standards: cribs, strollers, bike helmets, lighters, mattresses, children's sleepwear flammability, lead and phthalate limits, and others.
  • De minimis shipments — the mandate explicitly covers low-value direct-to-consumer import shipments too, which is where many small Amazon and TikTok Shop sellers live.

US manufacturers take note: if everything you sell is genuinely made in the USA, eFiling doesn't apply to you — but you still legally need CPCs for children's products, and Amazon will still ask for them. The document-management half of this problem is yours either way.

3. What data has to be filed?

At a high level, CPSC's PGA (Partner Government Agency) message set in ACE expects data points like:

  • Product identification (what the product is, model/SKU-level detail)
  • The certifier — the domestic manufacturer or importer certifying compliance
  • CPSC-accredited lab identity and test report references
  • Which CPSC rules/bans/standards the product was tested against, and test dates
  • Where the product was manufactured

The practical consequence: a folder of PDFs is no longer enough. Your customs broker needs structured, per-SKU data fields. Sellers who track compliance in spreadsheets and email threads are the ones who will feel this most.

4. Deadlines and enforcement: where things stand

The mandate took effect July 8, 2026. Here's the honest picture on enforcement:

  • CBP is currently in a warning phase — entries with missing certificate data are being flagged, not rejected. Nobody's shipment is being seized solely over eFiling data today.
  • That phase will not last forever. The mandate is final, and compliance-data examinations and holds are rising.
  • Marketplaces don't do warning phases. Amazon suspends listings over missing CPCs and lab reports with short deadlines and no appeal sympathy. That risk is immediate, not theoretical.

Bottom line: this is a "get ready now while it's cheap" situation, not a panic — but it's not going away.

5. Amazon, Walmart, and TikTok Shop have their own gates

Separate from customs eFiling, each marketplace runs its own document checks:

  • Amazon periodically demands CPCs and lab test reports per SKU/ASIN — sometimes many at once — with short compliance windows. Miss the window and the listing goes down.
  • Walmart Marketplace gates regulated categories at listing time and on review.
  • TikTok Shop requires product qualification documents for regulated categories before you can list.

Each wants the documents formatted their way, on their timeline. One organized vault per SKU beats three separate scrambles.

6. How a small seller gets ready (checklist)

  1. Inventory your catalog by category. List every SKU and which CPSC rules apply to it. Children's products = CPC; general-use products under a rule = GCC.
  2. Collect every certificate and test report. CPCs, GCCs, lab reports, accreditation letters — per SKU and variant. Note expiry/retest dates.
  3. Check what's missing or stale. This is where most small sellers find gaps — a CPC that references an old test report, a variant that was never certified, a lab that lost accreditation.
  4. Structure the data. Move from PDFs-in-a-folder to per-SKU records: certifier, lab, test date, rules tested, factory. This is what your broker will need for the PGA message set.
  5. Talk to your customs broker now. Ask what eFiling data format they want from you and when. Don't discover the answer during a live shipment.
  6. Set retest reminders. Annual retesting and certificate expiries are the silent killers — calendar them 90/60/30 days out.

7. Common questions

Do I need a lawyer for this?

Most small sellers don't — they need organized documents and a broker who knows the PGA message set. For genuinely ambiguous product-classification questions, the CPSC Small Business Ombudsman is free, and a qualified attorney beats internet advice. (CertVault is software, not legal advice.)

How much does compliance usually cost a small seller?

Lab testing runs hundreds per product, consultants charge $500–$2,000+ per project, and a single Amazon document scramble has cost sellers ~20 hours of labor. The expensive part is usually disorganization, not the documents themselves.

I'm a tiny seller with 10 SKUs. Does this really apply to me?

If you import regulated products — yes, including de minimis shipments. The mandate has no small-seller exemption. The good news: 10 SKUs is an afternoon of organization, not a project.

Where can I read the actual rule?

Start with the CPSC's eFiling page (cpsc.gov) and 16 CFR 1110. Your customs broker should also have a one-pager on their PGA filing process — ask them.
